A receptionist leaves at six, the accountant works late, and the cleaner comes at five in the morning — but the same metal key opens every door, and if a copy goes missing you are re-keying the whole building. Access control replaces that guesswork with doors that know who is allowed in, when, and a record of every entry. For offices, clinics, and hotels in Addis Ababa, it is no longer a luxury reserved for banks. The two locks at the heart of every system Almost every access-controlled door uses one of two locks. A magnetic lock (maglock) holds the door shut with an electromagnet — it releases the instant power is cut, which makes it the right choice for emergency exits and glass doors, because it always fails open. An electric strike replaces the plate in the door frame and releases the latch when access is granted — it keeps the door physically locked during a power cut, so it suits storerooms, server rooms, and cash offices where security matters more than exit speed. A good design often uses both, chosen door by door. The wrong choice is not just inconvenient — a strike on a fire exit or a maglock on a server room is a safety or security failure. How people actually get in Keypads and RFID cards are the everyday workhorses: a card or fob per employee, a shared PIN for low-risk doors. They are cheap to replace, and a lost card is deleted from the system in seconds — no locksmith, no new keys for twenty staff. Biometric readers (fingerprint or face) suit doors where a shared card would be abused, like drug stores in a hospital or the finance office, because the credential cannot be lent to a friend. For most offices we recommend cards for general doors and biometrics only where accountability really matters. The feature owners forget to ask about: the audit trail Every grant and denial is logged with a name and a timestamp. When stock goes missing or a door was open after hours, you stop arguing from memory and start reading a report. The audit trail also answers the awkward questions before they become disputes: who entered the store room on Sunday, and at what time. On our projects this log is what owners end up checking most — more than the lock itself. Power cuts and what happens when the lights go out This is the first thing to ask any installer in a city where power interruptions are a fact of life. A proper system runs its controller and locks from a battery-backed supply, so doors keep working for hours without mains. What matters is how each door behaves when the battery finally drains: fire-exit doors must fail open so people can get out, while high-security doors should fail locked so nobody gets in. If your installer cannot tell you, door by door, which behavior each lock has and why, the design phase is being skipped. It also opens doors for you Modern controllers integrate with the rest of the building: the same card that opens the front door can sign a PC in and out for attendance, a delivery at the gate can buzz reception over the intercom, and the CCTV system can bookmark the video of every door event, so reviewing "who came in last night" takes minutes, not hours of scrubbing footage. At Summit General Hospital, access control ties into the same network that carries the CCTV and the nurse call systems — one infrastructure, several jobs. What to ask your installer Ask to see the door schedule in writing: which lock type on which door, and whether it fails open or fails locked. Ask how long the battery backup actually lasts and how the system behaves when it runs out. Ask how a lost card is revoked and how new staff are added — and who holds the administrator password when the installer leaves. And ask for the audit log to be demonstrated on your own doors before handover, not on a demo screen. The bottom line Access control is not about making your office feel like a fortress. It is about knowing exactly who can go where, replacing lost keys with deleted cards, and sleeping better because every entry is on record. We design it door by door — maglocks, strikes, readers, battery backup and all — and hand over the documentation so the system stays yours, not the installer's.
