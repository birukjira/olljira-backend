Choosing a Hospital Management System is one of the most expensive decisions a hospital makes — and one of the easiest to get wrong. The demo always looks good. The problems show up six months after go-live, when the nurses are keeping paper backups and the X-ray images won't open in the patient record. We designed and delivered the network, IP phone system, access control, and HIMS infrastructure at Summit General Hospital, and this is the checklist we now walk hospital directors through before they sign with any vendor. 1. Start with patient workflow, not features Map how a patient actually moves: registration, triage, consultation, lab, imaging, pharmacy, billing, discharge. Then ask the vendor to demo exactly that journey, end to end, with no pauses. If the demo jumps between modules or the salesperson says "that part is configured later," you have found a gap your staff will live with daily. 2. Separate the acronyms and test the joins HMS runs administration — registration, billing, scheduling. EMR holds the clinical record — notes, histories, prescriptions. PACS stores and serves imaging — your X-ray and MRI studies. Vendors blur these lines because each module is a line item. The question that matters: does a doctor open the patient's X-ray from inside the EMR, or does she log into a second system with a second password? Integration between the three is where most deployments quietly fail. 3. Ask imaging the hard questions Is the PACS truly DICOM-compliant, and has it been connected to your specific X-ray and MRI machine models before — in Ethiopia, not just in the vendor's home market? Can radiologists report from a workstation, and can clinicians view studies from the wards? What happens to archived studies if you ever change vendors? Get export commitments in writing. 4. Check the infrastructure requirements honestly Every system claims to be "web-based." Ask instead: what happens when the internet drops — does the hospital stop, or does the system run on the local network and sync later? What server hardware is required on premises, and who maintains it? A hospital in Addis Ababa needs a clear answer on power cuts and connectivity, because both will happen. 5. Demand local support and training in the contract Who picks up the phone at 2 AM when the system is down and patients are waiting? How many reference hospitals in Ethiopia can you visit and call? Training should be itemized: how many days, for how many staff, and what happens when you hire new people six months later. A system your staff can't use confidently is an expensive filing cabinet. 6. Price the whole five years, not the first invoice License fees are the visible part. Add implementation, data migration from your current records, hardware, annual support, per-user growth, and module upgrades. Ask for a written five-year total cost of ownership.
