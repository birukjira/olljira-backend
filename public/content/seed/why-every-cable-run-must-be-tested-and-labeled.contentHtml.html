A network cable either passes its test or it doesn't — but you'd be surprised how many installations in Addis Ababa have never been tested at all. The link light comes on, the installer leaves, and six months later someone spends two days finding a fault that a five-minute test would have caught on day one. Testing is proof, not politeness A proper cable analyzer test — we use a Fluke DSX — measures wiremap, length, insertion loss, crosstalk, and return loss against the actual standard for the cable category. It produces a printed or PDF report per run. That report is the difference between "it worked when we left" and "here is documented proof that run 2F-14 meets spec." Labeling is the other half Every port should be labeled at both ends with a scheme a stranger can understand: floor, room, port number. When something fails at 8 PM on a Sunday — and failures always pick those hours — a labeled port map turns a two-hour trace into a two-minute fix. Your own staff can do it. No emergency callout, no guessing which of forty identical blue cables is the dead one. What to demand from any installer Before work starts: which cable category, which tester, and what documentation you will receive. During the work: watch whether runs are labeled as they go or "later" — later often means never. At handover: the test report for every run, a port map, as-built drawings, and a written workmanship warranty. Why we insist on it Most of our repair work starts at sites someone else installed — unlabeled racks, mystery cables, and no paperwork. The business pays twice: once for the original job, once to make it maintainable. Our rule is simple: if a run isn't tested and labeled, the job isn't done. That's not a premium service. It's the minimum a professional installation should include.
